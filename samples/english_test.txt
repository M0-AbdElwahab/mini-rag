The Great Pyramid of Giza

Overview

The Great Pyramid of Giza is the oldest and largest of the three pyramids on the Giza plateau, on the western edge of modern Cairo in Egypt. It was built as a tomb for the pharaoh Khufu, who ruled during the Fourth Dynasty of the Old Kingdom. Most scholars date its construction to around 2560 BC. It is the only one of the Seven Wonders of the Ancient World that still largely survives today.

Size and Structure

When it was finished, the pyramid stood about 146.6 metres (481 feet) tall. Erosion and the loss of its outer casing stones have reduced its height to roughly 138.5 metres. Each side of the base is about 230 metres long. The structure contains an estimated 2.3 million blocks of stone, most of them local limestone. For nearly 3,800 years it was the tallest man-made structure in the world, until the spire of Lincoln Cathedral in England was completed in the 14th century.

The outer casing was made of fine white limestone brought from the quarries at Tura, across the Nile. Granite for the King's Chamber came from Aswan, more than 800 kilometres to the south. Most of the casing stones were later removed and reused to build mosques and buildings in medieval Cairo.

Inside the Pyramid

The pyramid has three main chambers. The lowest is cut into the bedrock beneath the structure and is usually called the Subterranean Chamber. Above it are the Queen's Chamber and the King's Chamber, connected by a tall, narrow passage known as the Grand Gallery. The King's Chamber holds an empty granite sarcophagus. In 2017, a team using cosmic-ray imaging as part of the ScanPyramids project announced the discovery of a large hidden void above the Grand Gallery, about thirty metres long. Its purpose is still unknown.

Who Built It?

The ancient Greek historian Herodotus wrote that one hundred thousand slaves built the pyramid over twenty years. Modern archaeology does not support this. Excavations south of the pyramids uncovered a town with bakeries, breweries and housing for workers, along with workers' tombs. These findings suggest that the builders were organized Egyptian laborers who were fed and cared for, probably working in rotating teams.

In 2013, archaeologists at Wadi al-Jarf on the Red Sea coast found the oldest known inscribed papyri. They include the logbook of an official named Merer, who described transporting limestone blocks by boat to Giza during Khufu's reign.

Why It Still Matters

Today the Great Pyramid attracts millions of visitors every year and remains one of the most studied monuments in the world. Researchers continue to use new technology, from satellite imaging to muon scanning, to learn how it was designed and built. A new museum, the Grand Egyptian Museum, stands nearby and is designed to display the treasures of ancient Egypt, including objects from the tomb of Tutankhamun.
